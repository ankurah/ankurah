Members of an ankurah system are durable nodes that each keep a store of the system's entities, or of a part of it. They take commits independently, go offline, come back, and must end up holding the same events without any server that knows the truth. This page explains the mechanism proposed for that, in the order one needs to understand it. Everything in it applies unchanged whether the members hold a whole system or only a share of one; the last two steps say how.

Step 1: the problem it solves

Where we are. Two durable nodes, members of the same system, each hold a store of the same entities. They have been apart for a while, offline or partitioned, and each has taken commits the other has not seen. Now they reconnect. Replace "the same system" with "the same share" and the scene is identical for Alice and Bob, which is the point of the whole design: one mechanism, applied to a whole system or to one share's subset.

The question this scheme answers. When the two reconnect, how does each side learn exactly which entities differ, and fetch the missing events for those alone, while sending as little as possible, with no server that knows the truth, and with either store possibly holding millions of entities? Sending everything is out. Keeping a log of "what I sent whom" is out too, because a member may have taken commits from third parties while the other was away.

What to take from the picture. Five entities out of sixty differ, and neither side knows which five. The requirement the whole scheme is built around is that the network cost of finding them scales with five, the number of differences, not with sixty, the size of the stores; the depth of the search and the local scans on each side still grow with the store (step 3). At a million entities and a dozen differences that is the difference between a session that takes milliseconds and one that rescans everything. Everything that follows (the digest tree, the additive digest, the small-store encode, the size rule) is a way to buy that one property under different store sizes.

Step 2: what differs, for one entity: the leaf

Where we are. Zoom in on a single entity. In ankurah its state is the fold of its events, and the events form a DAG in which every event names its parent events by id. Those ids are hashes, so one event id pins down its entire ancestry. The question for this step: what is the smallest thing one member can tell the other so both know whether this entity differs, without sending the state or the events?

The answer is the head: the set of the entity's newest events, the ones with no children yet. Equal heads mean the same events and so the same state, under three conditions the scheme relies on: each store is ancestor-closed and durable, holding every ancestor of every head it stores, so a head names a history the store really has; the fold is deterministic, so the same events give the same state on every node; and both stores admitted the same events, since an event one node masks under its admission rules and the other does not changes the effective state without changing the head. Durable members meet the first condition by construction. An ephemeral node holding a state snapshot without its history meets it only by trusting the member that produced the snapshot, so its equal head certifies the snapshot's state no further than that trust. The leaf does not settle the third condition at all: admission evidence lies outside event ids and reconciles as its own stream beside the leaves (44-full-replica-reconciled.html, section 2, the invariants reconciled), which is why a successful session certifies equal leaves and never equal effective state (the close, in step 6). The unit the whole scheme compares per entity is therefore the leaf: the entity id with a hash of its sorted head, filed under the key the entity has in the index being compared (the precise form follows the picture). The picture shows the three things that can happen when two members compare one leaf.

What to take from the picture. Comparing one entity costs one leaf, on the order of a hundred bytes plus the key, because the head travels as a fixed-size hash, and a mismatch always has the same remedy: send the events the other side lacks, which the two heads make computable because every event names its parents. In the diverged case nobody wins or loses. Both members end up holding both newest events, and ankurah's existing event DAG merges them exactly as it does today. No version counters, no per-peer bookkeeping and no clocks are added to events by this scheme. The remaining problem is scale: with a million entities, a million leaves is still too much to send.

The leaf, precisely. A leaf is three things: the canonical key under which an index files the entity, which is ankurah's engine-independent encoding of the index key (on the entity-id index the key field is empty, since the entity id follows it; step 12 brings indexes with other keys); the 32-byte entity id; and the head hash, SHA-256 (the hash event ids already use) over its own domain tag org.ankurah.digest.head.v0 followed by the head's distinct event ids in ascending byte order. An index holds one leaf per distinct key-and-entity pair, so an entity filed under two keys is two leaves, and never two copies of one pair: the store enforces that uniqueness, and step 4 says why the digest depends on it. What a leaf list transmits is, per leaf, the key, the entity id and the head hash. A member that finds a leaf differing fetches the other side's full head, checks it against the head hash, and only then compares the heads through the event DAG. A head of many concurrent events can be long, so full heads travel in pages under the message size bound of step 6, never inside a leaf list. A key cannot be paged, so a per-index limit on key length keeps any single leaf under that bound.

Step 3: grouping leaves so one short digest stands for many: the digest tree

Where we are. Each side now has a leaf per entity, and a million leaves is still too much to send. The question for this step: how can one short message stand for a whole region of leaves, so that equal regions are skipped at once and only differing regions are looked into?

Entity ids are 32-byte hashes, so their bits are uniform. Group the leaves by the first four bits of the id (16 groups), then by the next four (256), and so on, a few levels deep, until a group holds a few dozen to a few hundred leaves. (The figures write each four-bit group as a hexadecimal digit so that prefixes have readable names; nothing depends on hexadecimal, and the number of bits per level is a tuning choice, see the note at the end of this step.) That bottom group is a bucket. Every group is a node, named by its index and its prefix. It carries a digest that stands for every leaf beneath it and the count of those leaves, two sums that add and subtract as leaves come and go. The commit that changes a leaf adds the difference to every node along the path, in its own transaction (step 5). Two members compare node by node. Equal digest and count mean the whole branch is identical and is skipped. A differing node is opened one level. The names are derived from the ids, so nobody allocates them, nothing becomes an entity or an event, and any member can rebuild the whole tree from its own leaves.

What to take from the picture. With sixteen-way branching, a single differing entity among a million costs one comparison per level plus one bucket of a few hundred leaves. So the network cost of a session grows with the number of differing branches times the depth, and with the store's size only through the depth, which grows as its logarithm; a check that finds no difference costs one root comparison at any size. Local work is another matter: scanning buckets, running the encode and maintaining the tree all grow with the store. The tree is bookkeeping each store keeps for itself: no member ever sends its tree, only the digests of the nodes a session asks about. One thing is still unexplained: how a node's digest is computed so that a changed leaf updates it cheaply, and so that two stores that built their trees in different orders agree on every digest. That is the next step.

Asked while reading: bits or hexadecimal, fixed shape or balancing, entities or not?

Three questions on this step, 2026-10-05: it does not matter that entity ids are hexadecimal, the tree should be over the binary representation, and if sixteen is optimal it is modulo whatever; is the proposal a fixed-size structure, and would a balancing tree make sense; and is the digest tree itself made of entities, with the pros and cons of that.

Bits, not hexadecimal. The tree is a radix tree over the id's bits: each level takes the next b bits of the id, and sixteen-way branching is simply b = 4. Hexadecimal is only how a four-bit group is written in the figures, so prefixes have readable names; nothing in the structure depends on it. Because ids are hashes, every level is expected to split its leaves nearly evenly, so b is a tuning choice: two bits per level gives a four-way tree seven levels deep at a million leaves, four bits gives sixteen-way and three or four levels, eight bits gives 256-way and two. The study's arithmetic chose sixteen (a node row with its sixteen children fits one page; the per-level cost of a commit stays small), but it is a per-store setting, and members need not even agree on it: any prefix's digest is the sum of whatever nodes or leaves lie beneath it, so a member with eight-bit levels answers a four-bit prefix by adding sixteen rows.

Fixed fan-out, depth by size, no balancing. So the proposal is a fixed-fan-out radix tree whose depth is chosen by the store's size, not a balancing tree, and it needs no balancing because hashed ids are uniform: at a million leaves no sixteen-way prefix is more than a few percent heavier than another. Balanced and content-defined trees (B-trees, Merkle search trees, prolly trees) buy adaptivity at the price of unstable names, since a split renames ranges, and names are what claims and hearsay refer to. Those structures exist to force two Merkle trees into the same shape, which the additive digest makes unnecessary: a node holds a cached sum, so two members never need the same shape to compare (step 4). Growth is a split: when buckets outgrow their target, a level is added by computing sixteen child rows from each bucket's leaves, nothing else rehashed. Two residual cases. An adversary can grind ids into one prefix: a k-bit prefix costs about 2k hash trials per accepted id, so shallow prefixes are cheap to crowd, including prefixes that hold honest entities. A session meets that by count-based splitting of the crowded range at key bounds the responder states, as Willow and Negentropy do, because any range's digest is computable, and by limits on depth and work (the last paragraph of this note). And a store that shrinks keeps a level it no longer needs, harmlessly.

Derived rows, not entities, and that is more than an optimization. The nodes are rows in a store-local table, named by their prefix, written only by the commit's fold, and never sent to anyone: sessions and claims carry digests, never nodes. A node's digest is a pure function of the store's leaves. As an entity it would be authored state carried by events, replicated to every member, and two members updating the same node at the same time would produce conflicting events for a value that must never be in conflict. It would also need ids to allocate, policy to say who may write it, and its own synchronization, which is circular. The honest cost of the derived form: one more table per storage engine plus the fold's work in each commit, and a tree invisible to models and queries. That invisibility is also why the audit is simply "recompute the root from the leaves and compare".

Depth per branch. Asked next: does it make sense for the tree to have dynamic branch depth? Yes, and it is the better reading of the proposal: depth per branch, by a split threshold, with nearly uniform depth as the case that hashed ids are expected to produce on their own when nobody grinds them. On the entity-id tree, when nobody grinds ids, occupancy is close to uniform and branches reach the split threshold at about the same size, so its depths come out nearly even; nothing prevents a ground prefix, though, which is why count-based splitting and the limits on depth and work exist. The index keyed by group id then entity id is a different matter: one group may hold ten entities and another a million, so a uniform depth is wrong for both at once, the small group's buckets standing nearly empty and the big group's overflowing. A bucket that splits when it passes its target, and a branch that stays shallow while it is light, handles that locally, and it also absorbs an adversary who grinds ids into one prefix locally; the wire-side count splitting and the limits on depth and work stay necessary all the same, because grinding is cheap for shallow prefixes and the simulations that looked reassuring used uniform keys. What it costs: growth becomes a local, amortized operation, a scan of one overfull bucket to compute its sixteen children, instead of a store-wide deepening step; finding a leaf's bucket becomes "the longest stored prefix of this key", a walk down the stored nodes from the root, one read per stored level, which the fold makes anyway because it appends a delta under every prefix on the path, rather than a fixed key computation; and members stop having identical node sets, which is where the additive digest already pays, because a session asks for a prefix's digest and each side computes it from whatever rows it keeps, children or leaves. Names stay derived, claims still name prefixes, and the sessions of steps 6 to 9 are untouched.

Incremental, never a rebuild; different depths in different parts. Asked next: does dynamic depth need a whole rebuild or is it incremental, and do different parts of the tree have different depths? A split touches one bucket: its leaves are scanned once to compute its sixteen children, and the new node keeps the bucket's digest and count unchanged, because the sum of the children is the sum of the leaves and nothing beneath it changed. Nothing above or beside it changes, and the next commit simply lands one level deeper. A merge, when a branch empties, is the reverse and equally local. A removal, an entity leaving the index or moving to another key, subtracts the leaf's point along the old path and deletes its folded row, and leaves nothing behind: no store remembers what a partner has seen, every session compares by digest from the roots down (step 6), and a removal the partner has not received shows as a differing digest like any other change. The only operations that rebuild are the audit (recompute the root from the leaves, on purpose) and a change of the bits-per-level setting, which is a per-store constant chosen once. The initial fill is incremental too: buckets split as they pass the target while the build fills the tree and commits fold into it. And yes, each branch is as deep as its own load requires: on the entity-id tree the depths come out nearly uniform in practice, because hashed ids that nobody grinds load the branches nearly alike; on the group-keyed index they come out ragged, a one-bucket branch beside a four-level one, which is the point.

Limits on splitting. A split is locally correct because the parent keeps its digest, and four limits keep splitting bounded. Path compression: a prefix with a single non-empty child keeps no row of its own, so a long run of shared key bits, such as a long common property value at the front of an index key, costs one row rather than one per level. A maximum depth per store: a bucket still over its target at that depth stays whole and is scanned, so keys ground or chosen to share a long prefix cannot drive splitting without end. Transaction boundaries: a split or a merge happens inside the commit's transaction whose change crossed the threshold, reading the bucket's leaves under that same transaction, so no reader ever sees half a split. And on the wire, count-based splitting names exact key bounds: the responder splits a crowded range at boundary keys it states, so both sides compute digests over the same sub-ranges whatever their own trees look like.

Step 4: the additive digest, and how a digest tree differs from a Merkle tree

Where we are. Every node needs a digest that stands for the leaves beneath it, a store takes commits all day, and two stores that built their trees in different orders must still agree on every digest. The question: how is a node's digest computed so that one changed leaf costs a few operations, the order of insertion does not matter, and nobody can produce an equal digest for a different set of leaves?

The grouping by prefix is the same idea as a Merkle tree's. The difference is the combining function. A Merkle node is the hash of its sixteen children in a fixed order: the grouping is part of what the value means, so the nodes define the digest, recomputing one needs all sixteen child hashes, and two members get the same hash for the same leaves only if they group them identically all the way down, by a canonical shape rule both keep. The proposed digest is additive. Each leaf, its key, entity id and head hash (step 2), is hashed to a point on an elliptic curve (ristretto255 is proposed, with the map given below the picture), and a node's digest is the sum of the points of every leaf beneath it. Addition commutes, so the order of arrival is irrelevant. A changed leaf is handled by subtracting its old point and adding the new one, and that same difference is added to every node on the path to the root without reading a sibling. A parent's digest is also the sum of its children's digests, so the root equals the plain sum of every leaf's point as if no tree existed, and a node is only a cached sum over a key range: any grouping of the same leaves gives the same digest for the same set, two members need not agree on depth or bucket size, and any key range has a digest, from whatever cached sums each member holds plus the leaves at its edges. The curve is there for one reason: with XOR, or with a compact modular sum such as a 64- or 128-bit AdHash (Bellare and Micciancio's sum of leaf hashes modulo an integer), an author who chooses events, and so the hashes being summed, can build a different set of leaves with the same digest at practical work by Wagner's generalized birthday attack, as the analysis in Maitin-Shepard, Tibouchi and Aranha's paper sets out, so equal digests would prove nothing. The curve's prime group order is what makes the sum collision-resistant: on it, the same authors reduce that forgery to a discrete logarithm, under their theorem's assumptions rather than unconditionally: the leaf-to-point map behaves as a random oracle built from an efficiently samplable encoding, the group has prime order, and the multiplicities in a colliding pair differ by less than that order, which unique leaves satisfy trivially.

What to take from the picture. A commit costs, per leaf it changes, one curve hash of the new leaf, one decode of the old leaf's stored point, one subtraction, and one encode of the difference, which it then appends under every prefix on the leaf's path (step 5), so an entity filed under several keys costs one leaf and one path per key (measured: 5.0 microseconds for the hash on ristretto255, 2.4 microseconds for a decode or an encode of the canonical compressed point, 66 nanoseconds per addition in memory; a reader of a node pays about 4.9 microseconds per base or delta row it decodes and sums; a Merkle node costs 1.0 microsecond to hash plus reading its sixteen children), and two stores holding the same leaves hold the same digest at every node no matter how they got there. That is what lets a digest comparison stand in for a leaf-by-leaf comparison. Said plainly, so that the design is defended for what it is: for synchronization alone, a Merkle tree over a canonical prefix trie would meet the same goal, two members finding the entities they differ on in a logarithmic number of exchanges, for any tree spec, and it would give three things this digest does not: post-quantum collision resistance from SHA-256, proof to a third party that a leaf is in the set, and self-healing, since a Merkle node written from stale inputs is corrected the next time anything beneath it changes, where an additive node that absorbed a change twice or missed one stays wrong until the audit rebuilds it. What the additive digest brings is in the bookkeeping of writes: a commit reads only the old leaf row and never a sibling; its cost does not grow with the number of concurrent committers, because commits to different entities never touch the same row, where every concurrent Merkle commit meets at the root and retries or waits; and nothing about bucketing is in the contract between members, only the leaf and the hash, where a Merkle tree's shape rule is part of the protocol and kept identical by every member for good. Those are the terms of the trade, and the conditional writes and the audit of step 5 are its price.

The leaf-to-point map, precisely. The map is RFC 9380's hash_to_ristretto255 (its appendix B), the random-oracle construction the security argument needs, which choosing a prime-order group does not supply by itself: the leaf's canonical bytes are expanded with expand_message_xmd over SHA-512, under the domain separation tag org.ankurah.digest.leaf.v0, taken as its literal ASCII bytes with no terminator (the form the repository's event ids already use for their own tag), to 64 uniform bytes, and RFC 9496's element derivation maps those bytes to a group element. The leaf's canonical bytes are three parts, each preceded by its length as eight big-endian bytes: the key, the 32-byte entity id and the 32-byte head hash. The head hash is step 2's, and its tag org.ankurah.digest.head.v0 is likewise its literal ASCII bytes with no terminator; the hash function normalizes a supplied head itself, sorting its event ids and removing duplicates, and a member verifies an advertised head hash against the fetched head before it uses the head. Under the assumptions above, the classical security level is approximate: ristretto255's order is about 2252, so a generic collision search takes about 2126 work, conventionally counted in the 128-bit class, and the 32-byte encoding alone does not give it. The tree needs set-collision resistance only, not resistance against arbitrary repetitions of a leaf (Clarke and colleagues separate the two), because a valid tree never holds a leaf twice: the folded table keys every leaf by its key and entity id, and a second copy is a broken store invariant that the audit reports, never a legitimate input. Stored points need a codec of their own. The uncompressed form that makes additions fast is the curve library's internal representation, private to the library and laid out differently on 32-bit and 64-bit targets, not a portable format; so rows store a version byte followed by the canonical 32-byte ristretto255 encoding, validated on decode. A commit decodes only the old leaf's point and encodes only the one difference it appends under each prefix of the path, so it never decodes a node; a reader decodes a node's base row and its delta rows once per read, and compaction once per delta it folds into the base (the measured addition is about 66 nanoseconds in memory and a decode or an encode about 2.4 microseconds; these measure different operations), with rows rebuilt when the encoding changes, since trees are derived state. The same canonical bytes are what enters a wire fingerprint, a claim or a signature, since one group element can have several internal representations but one canonical encoding.

Asked while reading: what is admitted to the tree, and does arrival order matter?

The question was whether the rule for adding an event id to the tree is "the event was not stored on this node, but is about to be", and whether the point is that the tree accommodates any order of arrival while giving the same result.

Close, with one correction that matters: the tree never holds event ids. It holds one leaf per key-and-entity pair, the key, the entity id and the hash of the entity's sorted head, and the event ids enter only through that head. So the admission rule is not "an event I do not have yet" but "a commit of mine that changes a leaf", an entity's head or its key in some index. Core's fold, inside that commit's transaction, reads the entity's folded rows for the old keys and points, takes the new head and keys from the state the commit writes, subtracts each old leaf's point and adds the new one, and appends each difference under every prefix of the paths. A duplicate delivery never becomes a commit, so it never touches the tree; an event the store already has contributes nothing.

The second point is the heart of it, and it holds at two levels, plus a third. The head of an entity is the frontier of its DAG, a function of which events the store holds and not of the order they arrived in. The digest is a sum, so the order in which entities were folded does not matter either. And a sum is shape-free, so how the tree was built does not matter. Two members holding the same events under the same index identity, with the same derived memberships and the same admission, therefore hold the same canonical leaves and so the same digest at every node, which is the whole reason a comparison works without a shared clock.

What to take from the picture. Nothing is ever "added" to the tree in the sense of a growing set of events. The tree is a function of the store's current leaves, and a commit replaces one leaf. That is also why the tree can always be rebuilt from the folded rows alone, and why the audit in the proposal is simply "recompute the root and compare".

Asked while reading: addition is not idempotent, so must the tree track what it has added?

The question, 2026-10-05: the main limitation of elliptic-curve addition is that it is not idempotent, so we have to keep track of what has already been added to which tree, right?

Exactly right, and it is the reason the design keeps two pieces of bookkeeping beside the tree. Point addition counts: adding a leaf twice counts it twice, and removing a leaf you never added leaves garbage behind, so a tree is only correct if every commit is applied to it exactly once, with the old leaf subtracted and the new one added. The folded row answers "what is in this tree for this entity": one row per key-and-entity pair per tree, which is one per entity except where an index files an entity under several keys, holding the key, the head hash and the leaf's current point. The commit reads it to know what to subtract, and the stored point saves the five-microsecond hash of the old leaf; without that row, subtraction would be impossible and a replay would double-add. The conditional write answers "has this change already been applied": the commit replaces the row only if it still holds what the commit read, inserts one only where none exists, and deletes one only if it still holds what was read, so a change applied twice finds the row already replaced and is refused, and the refusal fails the commit as a conflict, like a stale expected head, to be retried from fresh reads. Atomicity ties the two together: the folded rows and the node deltas are written inside the commit's own transaction (step 5), so a crash in the middle rolls back the commit and the tree alike, and nothing is ever half-applied. That is why the tree lives inside the store rather than beside it. An idempotent structure, a plain set of leaves, would not need the bookkeeping, and it supports deletion as well, but it has no compact summary whose parts compose: the point of the digest is thirty-two bytes per node that add up across any grouping or range, and that summary costs exactly-once application, which is bookkeeping a store already knows how to do. One consequence that is a feature: the same entity under two groups in the group-keyed index is two leaves and two folded rows, counted twice, with the count field tracking it; a Selection returns that entity once, which is why reusing such a range carries a condition of its own (the last note of step 12).

What to take from the picture. Non-idempotence is real and is paid for with ordinary store bookkeeping: a row per key-and-entity pair per tree, written conditionally with the node deltas in the commit's transaction. Step 5 is that machinery in full.

Asked while reading: so each entity needs a record of the trees it has been committed to?

The question, 2026-10-05: presumably a committed tree-ids list, or something like that, needs to be stored for each entity in the storage engine somehow?

Yes, and it is exactly that, with one refinement: not a list on the entity but a row per key-and-entity pair per tree, kept by the storage engine under its contract, with the entity's own state rows untouched. The folded row is the committed record. For each tree, the engine keeps a folded table with one row per key the entity is filed under in that index (its id; or its group then its id, one row per group): the key, the head hash and the leaf's current point. Beside it the engine keeps a lookup from entity id to the keys the entity has in that index, because the folded table is ordered by key while a commit names only the entity. "Which trees hold entity X" is "which keys the lookup lists for X", and "what each tree holds for X" is what those rows say; an entity in no group has no row in the group-keyed index. A key change is a move: when X's group changes from A to B, the lookup gives the old key, the subtree to subtract the old leaf from, and the state the commit writes gives the new key, where to add the new one; without the lookup the fold could not find the old leaf. Per tree, then, the folded table, the lookup, the node table (prefix to digest and count, as a base row and the delta rows appended since its last compaction) and the tree's build state, all written inside the commit's transaction. That, with the fold of step 5 on the commit's transaction, the index hooks and the registry (the appendix), is the whole of what the storage engine contract gains, and the folded and node tables are what the microbenchmark measured on SQLite and sled (the node row the microbenchmark measured held the digest uncompressed; the implementation holds it compressed, 41 bytes with the version byte and the count, so the row is smaller and an update through it costs about 4.9 microseconds; the lookup from entity to keys was not measured).

Asked while reading: should the tree be built into each query index, or stand beside it?

The question, 2026-10-05: does it make sense to build the digest tree into each query index, including the id index, or should it be a parallel structure, and why? And since building a query index and building a digest tree are both table scans, should both be done at once?

Parallel, and the reasons are concrete rather than aesthetic. The host engine owns its index pages: on SQLite, PostgreSQL and sled we cannot annotate B-tree interior pages with digests, and only an engine we wrote ourselves could carry them inside the index, so "into the index" is not available where it would matter most. Both update in the commit, by the ruling of 2026-10-08, but by different hands: the host engine maintains its index as it always has, and core's fold maintains the tree through the conditional map writes the engine's transaction offers; embedding would put core's curve arithmetic inside the engine's index pages, or the engine's index maintenance inside core. They share a key order, not a shape or a row: the index is the engine's B-tree over the key, the tree is a radix tree over the key's bits with depth per branch, and its leaf carries the head hash and the point, which no index entry needs; hashing is also chosen per index, opt-in by the ruling of that time (superseded by the next note: default on, opt-out per index), which a parallel table makes trivial and an embedded one makes all-or-nothing. And each can be rebuilt alone: the audit recomputes the tree from the folded rows without touching the index, and an index rebuild never touches the tree. The honest cost is that the folded table is in effect a second ordered structure over the same key, which the benchmark already paid for on SQLite and sled; where we own the keyspace, as on sled, the folded rows can sit adjacent to the index entries under a prefix for locality, a layout choice rather than a design change.

On the scan: yes, that tracks for the initial build. One pass over the entities computes each entity's key, head and point and fills the index and the folded table together, and the tree's node rows follow from the folded rows by splitting as buckets fill. Two refinements: the pass is dominated by the curve hash (57 percent of the 90-second build at ten million leaves), so the index comes almost free inside the tree's build rather than the other way round; and after the build the same commit maintains both, the engine its index and core's fold the tree.

Asked while reading: one tree per index, created with the index?

The question, 2026-10-05: is the intent to have one elliptic digest tree per index, created at the same time, assuming index auto-creation from push-downable predicates is added to PostgreSQL and SQLite as sled and IndexedDB already have it?

Yes, with three riders that turn the intent into a rule. One tree per index, default on, opt-out per index: every index a durable node keeps gets a tree registered with it, the entity-id index without exception, and an index may be excluded when nobody will ever compare across it. Ephemeral nodes keep trees too (a later ruling the same morning, see the note under step 12): over whatever they hold, with their prefixes rather than their roots comparable with a durable node’s. The cost of each extra tree is one folded row and one path of node rows per changed entity per commit, paid in the commit itself (the 5-microsecond hash and about five microseconds per node row), and a folded table beside the index, which is why opt-out exists rather than opt-in. The tree's key is ankurah's, not the engine's: the native index stores the engine's own key bytes, and SQLite's, PostgreSQL's and sled's differ, so the folded table's key must be a canonical encoding of the index key followed by the entity id, identical on every engine, or two members on different engines could never compare a prefix; this is one more reason the tree stands beside the index. Creation follows the index, by the ruling of 2026-10-08: the engine fires a hook where it creates an index (sled and IndexedDB auto-create one when a query's plan wants it; SQLite and PostgreSQL will, when their auto-created property indexes land), and core registers one tree per key spec in the registry of durable trees and builds it from entity state, from which the keys derive, with each entity's head (how membership enters that key is the discussion task of 2026-10-08, since every engine materializes members only and sled leads its index keys with the model), and the build runs under the same lock an index build takes on that engine, for an auto-created index as the same scan that fills the index, so commits wait for it as they wait for a backfill and no leaf is added twice or added back; the tree is published as ready when the build ends and is not comparable before. An index the engine drops fires the other hook, and the tree goes with it. Building in small batches that lock only their own entities is a later optimization, filed as its own task, ruled on 2026-10-11. With auto-creation on PostgreSQL and SQLite from push-downable predicates, each such predicate's index arrives with its tree, which is exactly what makes the offline checks of the step 12 notes available for the queries the application actually runs.

Step 5: who updates the tree, and when: the commit

Where we are. A member commits events all day and the tree must follow. The question: when and by whom are the folded rows and node rows updated, and what does that cost a commit?

The commit that changes a leaf updates the tree, inside its own transaction, the way an engine maintains a secondary index. Core's commit path does the fold itself, with no callback from the engine. It already holds the engine's transaction, on which it writes the entity rows, the events, the memberships and the materializations; before it commits, it derives each tree's keys for each written state (membership and the key spec's parts for an index tree; the predicate and the order by for a tree of its own, the note on the tree spec under step 12), turns the head into its hash, reads the entity's old folded rows to find what to subtract, computes each changed leaf's difference, and issues the tree writes on that same transaction: the folded rows, each conditional on the row it read, and one delta row appended under every prefix of each changed leaf's path from the root to the bucket; then it commits. The engine buffers those writes with the commit's own and applies them at COMMIT, so entity rows and tree rows become visible together or not at all: a conflict on an expected head returns before any tree write, a failed condition on a tree row fails the commit as a conflict, and a crash anywhere before the commit leaves neither. The engine never derives a key and never touches a curve point; the tree writes ride the commit's transaction, and nothing opens a second one. Anything that changes a leaf without a commit on the entity folds too, in the transaction of that change: a derived group membership change on the Zanzibar path (step 12), where moving a containing entity changes the keys of every entity beneath it, and a catalog or relation change that changes keys.

What a commit pays: one leaf hash (five microseconds), one folded row, and one delta row per node on the path from the root to the leaf's bucket, per changed entity per tree. The node rows are not rewritten in the commit, by the project lead's ruling of 2026-10-08 evening: core's commit path issues "append this delta under this prefix" and the engine inserts a delta row whose key ends in a suffix unique to that append, so nothing in the commit reads a node row, two commits under one node never contend, and the root is never a row that every commit fights over. A node's digest is its base row plus the sum of its delta rows, the same addition the tree uses everywhere; core compacts in the background, in transactions of its own: it reads the base and the deltas, writes the new base only if the base is still the one it read, and deletes each delta only if it is still the one it read, so a delta that lands during compaction is never lost and waits for the next round. A reader pays for the deltas accrued since the last compaction, which the compactor keeps few. The engine's part is an ordered byte map per tree, a read by key, a bounded range scan and four writes, insert-if-absent, put-if-current-equals, delete-if-current-equals and the append, with no tree vocabulary in it; the crate owns the maps for folded rows, the entity-to-keys lookup, nodes and build state on top. An earlier draft of this step folded the tree later, in batches, from a commit log, to keep this cost off the commit; the project lead withdrew it on 2026-10-08, because the exactness it gave up, a tree current only as of a fold position with its own durability fence, recovery rules and racing folders, cost more machinery than the batching saved; and read-modify-write of node rows inside the commit was rejected the same evening, because without core running inside the engine's commit it cannot hold the transaction's locks, and the engine must not do curve arithmetic.

Positions are dropped, by the project lead's ruling early on 2026-10-09. A database does not tell you which of two concurrent commits happened first; that order would have to be manufactured, on PostgreSQL by a lock every commit queues on, and each job positions did is done by the digests or by a conditional write: a session skips every subtree whose digest matches, so no watermark is needed; the live-delivery stream is registered before the session's snapshot and may carry a commit the snapshot already holds, which the repair rule tolerates; equal roots at the closing check are the acknowledgment; compaction deletes exactly the deltas it read; a change applied twice is refused by the row it finds. Tombstones go with them, since their only job was naming removals since a position. What the design assumes instead is that transactions roll back and run concurrently, two nodes on one database included: every tree write of a commit is in that commit's transaction, and every write is conditional on what the commit read, insert-if-absent for a new row, put-if-current-equals and delete-if-current-equals for a changed or removed row and for compaction, and the append for a delta; a failed condition fails the commit as a conflict, like a stale expected head, and core retries from fresh reads. A delta's key ends in a suffix the engine makes unique to that append, since one commit may change two leaves beneath one node.

What to take from the picture. A tree is always exactly as current as the state beside it. A session compares trees as they stand at each side's latest commit, never as of a lag, and the cost of that is paid by every commit, on its own path, about one delta row per tree level per changed entity.

Step 6: a session between two large stores

Where we are. Both members have taken commits while apart, and they reconnect. The question: what messages cross, in what order, and how many round trips does it take?

First, the side that opens the session names its scope explicitly: the index identity, which is the index's KeySpec and the canonical key encoding, and the range bounds; for a full replica, the entity-id index and its whole key range, whose cover (the note on the cover under step 12) is the one block at the root. Each side then sends the digest of every block of the cover, here its root: the digest at full width, the leaf count, and random bytes that both sides combine into a salt for the session. Equal roots end the session. Otherwise each sends the sixteen children of the root, digest and count each, with the digests below the root truncated to eight salted bytes, and the two compare. For every child that differs, the same exchange repeats one level down. At a bucket, the two exchange its leaf list, per leaf the key, the entity id and the head hash, and for every leaf that differs, each fetches the other's full head, checks it against the head hash, and sends the events the other lacks. The session closes after the repair by comparing every root of its scope at full width, so that a descent cannot end with a silent miss; that close checks the comparison, and proves nothing about what the peer holds.

What to take from the picture. A handful of differences in a million-entity store costs about one round trip per level plus two, a few kilobytes of digests, and the differing events themselves. Nothing is kept about the other member afterwards except the record of a successful close, which coverage status consults (step 12): the next session with the same partner starts from the roots like the first, and its cost is again set by the differences, not by how long the two were apart, since equal digests skip whole branches whatever happened beneath them meanwhile.

Turn-taking, precisely. The side that opens a session owns it: it sends every request and the other side answers. A request carries the session identifier, which both sides derive from their random bytes, a request number, and a list of explicit ranges, each a bit prefix of the scope's key or a pair of exact key bounds, with what it wants for each: the child summaries at a stated child width in bits (four for sixteen children, since members need not share a fan-out), a page of the range's leaves, or a batch of coded symbols (step 7); one request may mix the three. The request carries the owner's own summaries for those ranges and the answer carries the other side's, under the same identifiers, so after each answer both sides know which ranges differ, and the owner's next request names their children: one round trip per level, as in figure 6. A member that wants answers of its own opens its own session. Every message stays under a size bound both sides state when the session opens; a leaf list longer than one message travels as pages, each page an explicit key range carrying its own digest and count, so the receiver checks each page by itself against its own leaves in that range, and a bucket's pages must sum to the bucket's digest. A broken session resumes from its pending ranges, which are ranges of the key rather than stored nodes and so stay meaningful when either tree has split or merged meanwhile; the resumed session draws a fresh salt, and the close covers anything that changed in between.

The salt. Each side sends its random bytes, a nonce of 32 bytes, in its first message; in every derivation below, the opener's nonce comes first. The session identifier is SHA-256 over the two nonces in that order. The salt is SHA-256 over the session identifier and the scope (the system, the KeySpec and the bounds). Each eight-byte fingerprint is the first eight bytes of SHA-256 over the salt, the node's range (its prefix bytes and bit length, or its two bounds) and its full-width digest in the canonical compressed form of step 4, so a fingerprint is bound to one session and one range. An honest side's digests within a round are those of one read snapshot; the salt makes a collision prepared for another session worthless, and it authenticates nothing. Two different digests agree by chance with probability about 2-64 per comparison. The truncation saves bytes and catches accidents; it does not stop a peer from working adaptively once the salt is known, and like the close it never proves what the peer holds: a peer can echo an honest digest while withholding data, which no reconciliation detects, or force retries, which the budget below bounds. Stored digests, roots, the close and every claim stay at full width.

The close, precisely. Every session, a descent or an encode, over an index tree's cover or over a tree of its own (step 12), runs through the same five stages in order. It opens: each side states its scope and its random bytes. It compares: descent, leaf pages and coded symbols, with every answer of a round read from one read snapshot where the answering side's engine offers one. A side that can no longer serve the snapshot its round began on says so, and the round restarts with a fresh salt, so pages and coded symbols read from different snapshots are never mixed in one round; where an engine offers no snapshot, a commit that lands mid-round shows as a mismatch at the close and starts another round. It repairs: each side fetches what it lacks, and the receiving side makes the events durable before anything else. It refolds: each side's commits of the repaired data fold it into its trees. It closes: the two compare, at full width, the digest of every root of the comparison scope, meaning every block of the cover or the root of a tree of its own, whether the session descended or only ran the encode. Under the assumptions of step 4, equal full-width roots imply equal leaves beneath them, so the nodes a session touched need no check of their own. A successful close therefore certifies equal leaves under the agreed scope, equal heads for equal keys, and never equal effective state, which also needs equal admission: the admission-evidence stream of the attested design (step 11) reconciles that separately (step 2), and until that design, members of one system are assumed to run the same admission rules. Equal roots complete the session, and only then does either side record the completion (step 10). A mismatch, from a chance agreement at a truncated level, a commit that landed during the session, or a lying partner, starts another round with a fresh salt; when the session's budget of rounds and work runs out, it ends unreconciled, nothing is recorded, and no coverage status improves (the note on ephemeral nodes under step 12).

Step 7: small stores: the rateless encode

Where we are. A store with up to about a hundred thousand leaves. Its whole leaf list is a few megabytes, and a descent spends round trips a small store does not need. The question: can two small stores find their differences in one round trip without sending whole leaf lists?

Two words first. Rateless means the sender keeps producing coded symbols with no size fixed in advance, and the receiver tells it to stop as soon as it has decoded. A bucket is the group of leaves under the deepest prefix a store keeps a node row for, a few dozen to a few hundred leaves. The code works on fixed-size items, because a variable-length key or head cannot be combined this way: each leaf becomes one 96-byte item: the SHA-256 of its key, then its 32-byte entity id, then its 32-byte head hash. Each coded symbol combines a pseudo-random subset of the sender's items: the exclusive-or of the items, a signed 64-bit count, big-endian, and the exclusive-or of their checksums, each an eight-byte keyed SipHash-2-4 of the item under a key both sides derive from the session: the first sixteen bytes of SHA-256 over the session's salt (step 6) and the ASCII label "symbols". Which symbols an item joins is the mapping of the rateless paper (Yang, Gilad and Alizadeh), seeded by the item's keyed checksum: each item joins the symbols at the indices that mapping draws from the seed, with the paper's geometric spacing. The receiver computes the same symbol over its own items and subtracts it, so only the items that differ between the two remain, the sign of the count saying which side holds each. A symbol left holding exactly one item reveals it once validated, its count plus or minus one and its checksum equal to the keyed hash of the item it holds; removing that item from every other symbol reveals more, and the peeling continues until every symbol is empty. An item decoded twice, or decoded as held only by the sender when the receiver holds it too, marks the decode as corrupt. After decoding, the receiver fetches the full leaves of the decoded items by entity id and verifies each item's key hash and head hash against the leaf it receives. Measured: 1.36 to 1.74 symbols per differing item on average, means over trials from ten to ten thousand differing items (1.79 at four, where single trials ran to nine), and no decode failures in 2,340 trials. A changed entity is two differing items, its old leaf on one side and its new leaf on the other, and an entity one side lacks is one, so with the 80-byte symbols the benchmark used, at about 1.5 symbols per item, a changed entity costs about 240 bytes; the 96-byte items make symbols 112 bytes and, by the same arithmetic, about 340 bytes per changed entity, not yet measured. The price is a scan of every leaf per session, 0.2 to 0.6 microseconds per entity from the folded table.

What to take from the picture. One round trip for the encoding exchange, the opening, the repair and the close aside, and network cost proportional to the differences, paid for by a full scan of the sender's leaves, which is why this is for small stores, or for one differing branch of a large one.

Budgets, failures and trust. The 2,340 trials measure one workload; if they are independent and representative of it, they bound its failure rate to roughly 0.13 percent at 95 percent confidence. Their stopping budget was the benchmark's symbol cap, to be restated when the measurement is redone. They are not a guarantee, since a finite stream can stall and a checksum collision can corrupt a decode. So the receiver caps the symbols it accepts and the decoding work it spends, and when it reaches either cap the outcome is "the encode did not decode", which says nothing certain about how many items differ, never "more than k differ"; the pair then asks for more symbols within the cap or falls back to a descent. A decode that completes is accepted only when the full-width close of step 6 matches after the repair. The keyed checksum catches accidental collisions and items prepared without the key; it does not defend against a malicious session participant, who knows the key, so against a participant only the caps and the full-width close protect, as in any session.

Sources for this step: Goodrich and Mitzenmacher 2011; Eppstein, Goodrich, Uyeda and Varghese 2011; Yang, Gilad and Alizadeh 2024 and their riblt library; the earlier set-reconciliation bound of Minsky, Trachtenberg and Zippel 2003. Full references at the end of the page.

Step 8: choosing between descent and encode: the size rule

Where we are. Both tools exist and use the same tree. The question: how does a member choose, and does the choice depend on anything but its own store?

The rule is by leaf count. Below about a hundred thousand leaves: the per-session encode and no descent. Between a hundred thousand and a million: compare the top levels of the tree, then run the encode inside each branch that differs, where a branch of a few thousand leaves scans in microseconds. Above a million: the full descent, with truncated digests below the root, the closing check, and residency claims to choose whom to ask. The boundaries were computed from assumed read and scan costs. The microbenchmark has since measured those inputs on SQLite, near the assumptions, and on sled, whose reads are up to twenty times slower, which moves both boundaries up; the boundaries themselves are not yet recomputed. They are hypotheses until they are recomputed with the final mechanism running, the key-bound leaf, several index trees, realistic head sizes, IndexedDB, the closing checks, the scans on both sides, hot groups and split work, and until then they stay configurable.

What to take from the picture. One tree, three ways to walk it. The rule only picks how much of the tree a session touches, and it needs no agreement between members: whichever side owns the session may ask, range by range, for a root, for children, for a page of a bucket, or for coded symbols, and the other answers from the same tree.

Step 9: first contact: an empty store

Where we are. A new member joins, or a peer accepts a share. It holds nothing, so every node differs. The question: how does the first fill avoid a pathological session that opens every branch only to find everything missing?

The digest exchange is skipped when a count is zero. The empty side first records its source: the source member's identity and its incarnation, the identifier a store mints when it is created and again when it is reset. It then asks for the store wholesale, prefix by prefix: the leaf list and the events of prefix 0…, then 1…, and so on, recording each prefix as complete in the same transaction that makes that prefix's admitted events durable, so a recorded completion never runs ahead of the data. A crash resumes at the first incomplete prefix, from the same source and incarnation; if the source's incarnation has changed, the recorded completions no longer describe anything the source holds, and the fill continues as an ordinary session instead. The commits of the arriving events build the tree through core's fold (measured: a tree over ten million leaves builds in 90 seconds on one thread). The source keeps taking commits while later prefixes are copied, so an entity can land in a prefix already recorded as complete, and the completion record alone cannot repair that. So when every prefix is complete, the new member's tree holds everything received, and the two compare roots at full width: equal roots end first contact, and a mismatch starts an ordinary session (step 6) over the differences, never a fresh copy. Only after that close succeeds does the new member record its coverage of the scope as complete, the coverage its claims may assert (step 11). The same path serves a member that has fallen so far behind that a descent would open most of the tree.

What to take from the picture. First contact is a stream, not a search, resumable by prefix, verified afterwards by the same digest the ordinary session uses, and finished by an ordinary session when the source changed during the copy.

Step 10: between sessions: live deliveries

Where we are. After a session the two members are equal and stay connected, and commits keep happening. The question: does every commit start a session?

No. While connected, each member forwards to the other every event it stores that falls within the other's interest, which is ankurah's subscription path today: its own commits and, equally, the commits it receives from third members, so a third member's commit that reaches one side reaches the other without a session. What falls within an interest follows the repair rule of a Selection (the note on live queries under step 12): a side forwards every commit about an entity the other holds under the interest, including the commit that takes it out of the result, every commit that brings an entity into it, and, for a limited Selection, the replacements the limit admits. The stream is registered before a session takes its snapshot, so no commit falls between the compared tree and the stream: a commit that lands after the registration travels on the stream, one that lands before it is in the snapshot, and one that does both arrives twice, which the repair rule tolerates, since an event a store already has contributes nothing. Registering the stream before the snapshot fixes only the order of the two steps, not a moment on a clock. The stream carries its own identifier and the sender's incarnation, so every acknowledgment names the stream and the store it refers to. While connected, the receiver acknowledges each delivered commit by its id once it is durable on its side, and the sender retries what is unacknowledged; nothing counts commits, so there is no cursor to keep, no watermark to send through the commits a filtered stream left out, and nothing to void on a reset. A session is only the catch-up after a gap: a disconnection, a crash, a store reset, or a stream that could not deliver; after any of these, the digests decide what is missing.

What to take from the picture. The tree is for gaps, live forwarding is for the common case, and neither depends on the other being right.

Step 11: who holds what: residency claims

Where we are. More than two members, some holding only part of the system. A member that lacks a branch, or suspects it is behind, needs to know whom to ask. The question: how do members learn who holds what, without a server that keeps a roster?

A claim is a small statement about one scope of one holder's store, signed once keys are bound to holders (below). It carries the system; the index, as its KeySpec and canonical key encoding, or the Selection it describes; the node or range; the holder; the digest, compressed (step 4), and the count; the holder's incarnation and a revision number; the coverage the holder asserts for that scope, either none or complete, complete meaning its first fill closed successfully (step 9) and no gap it knows of has opened since; an expiry; and the signature over all of these in one canonical byte layout, which this page does not specify (below). In words: "I hold prefix a3… of this index, complete, at digest D, revision r of my incarnation i, until time t". The revision number is kept per holder and scope and raised on any change of the claim's other fields: a new digest after a commit, coverage falling after a known gap, an expiry renewed. Between two claims by the same holder for the same scope, precedence is by incarnation, then revision: a claim from any incarnation but the holder's current one is stale, and an expired one is ignored. Which incarnation of a holder is current is learned from the holder itself over a connection, never inferred from the incarnation's identifier. The rest of a claim's lifecycle is settled in the claims work, the last item of the order of work in the appendix. A revision ranks claims only within one holder's incarnation, never across holders. Members pass claims around as hearsay, over the same connections that carry events. Claims steer: whom to ask for a branch, whether a session is worth starting, which of a holder's claims is newest. Claims never decide: a member believes a branch equal only after exchanging digests with the holder itself. A holder's revision is its own local counter, so claims add nothing to events.

What to take from the picture. Hearsay for routing, evidence for truth, and the same tree names both.

What a signature can and cannot carry. A verified signature binds a statement to a key; it proves neither that the holder has the data, nor that its copy is complete, nor that the claim is still current, which is why claims stay routing hints. Even that binding needs a minimal identity link first: a way for a member that receives a claim relayed to know which key speaks for which holder, which transport authentication between two peers does not give a third. The minimal link is a signing key per holder, bound to the member by its roster entry; ankurah keeps no such key today (its nodes authenticate connections, not statements), so the key is new state, and a member verifies a signature against the bound key before it uses a signed claim at all. Settling the link belongs to the attested design that 90-replication-design-draft.html section 5 defers, and signed claims wait for that design. The canonical byte layout a signature covers, and the authorization a member needs to relay a claim, are likewise deferred to the claims work and the attested design and are not specified here; claims stay routing hints either way. Until then a claim is authenticated only by the connection it arrived on, which suffices for routing, and a partial replica shared outside a trusted pair waits for the trust tier that design provides.

Step 12: a share is one prefix of one index

Where we are. Back to Alice and Bob. Bob's store holds project C only, Alice's holds A, B and C, so their trees over entity ids differ almost everywhere, and nothing above would let them compare just C. The question: how do two stores that hold different subsets compare exactly the part they share?

Two words first. A group is the set of entities one share grants: everything filed under one root entity, such as project C. The group id is that root entity’s id; the word “group” is used here rather than “root” because “root” already names the top node of the tree. Build the tree over an index keyed by the group id first and the entity id second. An index here is the usual thing: a sorted list of rows, each row a key followed by the entity it names. With the group’s id as the first part of the key and the entity’s id as the second, every row of one group sits together in the sorted order, and that run of rows is what “the prefix C” means. The share's whole set is then one prefix, C's id, and both members compute the same digest and count for that prefix whatever else they hold. A session over a share is the ordinary session rooted at that prefix instead of the root. On the Zanzibar path the group id in the key is the derived row (root, entity) that the serving node maintains from the declared containment relation, not an authored tag, so the tree follows the relation without the entity carrying anything. A change to the relation, moving a containing entity for instance, changes the group keys of every entity beneath it without a commit on any of them, so the serving node runs core's fold, in the same transaction as the change, for each affected entity with its new keys, and the fold moves those leaves exactly as it would in a commit (step 5). Because a leaf includes its key, the move changes both groups' digests even though no head changed.

What to take from the picture. Partial replication reuses every step above unchanged. What the Share adds is which prefix a session is rooted at, and what the serving node adds is the derived rows that put each entity under its group.

Sources for this step and its notes: range-based set reconciliation, Meyer 2022, and the Willow protocol; Negentropy (nostr NIP-77) for fingerprints over filtered sets; Zanzibar, Pang and colleagues 2019, for the sharing side. Full references at the end of the page.

Asked while reading: one tree per predicate?

The question was whether "an interest named as prefixes of some hashed index" means a separate digest tree over the matching entities for each predicate that represents a partial replica's interest.

No. One tree per index, never one per predicate. An index is a sorted list of rows, each row a key followed by the entity it names, and the key may have several parts: say genre, then flavor, then the entity's own id. In the sorted order every row that shares a key prefix sits together, so "genre = jazz" is one run of rows and "genre = jazz and flavor = sweet" is a shorter run inside it. The tree over that index has one subtree per such run, and a member's interest, as far as one tree serves it, is simply the set of prefixes it holds, of whatever depth (the last note of this step widens an interest to any Selection; a set of prefixes is the case a tree serves directly). Every member with an interest in jazz and sweet, whatever else it holds, computes the same digest for that prefix from its own tree, so every interest of the form "genre = X" shares the one tree. A share is one instance of this: an index keyed by the group's id first, so one share is one prefix. A predicate that is not a prefix of any index tree gets no index tree: it gets a tree of its own over its result (the later notes of this step), and both sides compare those. Which indexes carry trees is one of the rulings, because each tree costs the commit one more folded row and path per changed entity. One detail: a leaf of such an index is the canonical key, the entity id and the head hash (step 2), so an entity that appears under two keys is two leaves and two folded rows.

What to take from the picture. The tree does not know about predicates at all. It knows one key order, and an interest, as far as a tree serves it, is a set of ranges in that order. That is why the Share names a root (the prefix) and a declared relation (what puts entities under that prefix), and why "which indexes are hashed" is a ruling rather than something each predicate decides for itself.

Asked while reading: are all of Alice's blue records as fresh as all of Bob's?

The question, for nodes that hold arbitrary subsets and promise nothing: how can Alice and Bob know, continuously and from an exchange much smaller than their stores, whether every record in Alice's live query "color = blue" is at least as fresh as every record in Bob's, without sending the full result sets, let alone the databases?

It is the same three pieces again, plus one cheap addition, which the later notes of this step fold into the one tree mechanism: the running digest below is the root of the result's own tree, root-only while the result is small. The addition is a running digest per live query: a live query's result set is already maintained incrementally by the reactor, so each side keeps the additive digest of its result alongside, one point added when an entity enters, one subtracted when it leaves, and on a head change the old point subtracted and the new one added, with the result membership and the digest changed in one step, so that a full recomputation over the result matches after every change the reactor makes. Its leaves are the result's entities as key-bound leaves of the index the interest names in its scope, each with the canonical key computed from the entity's state under that index's KeySpec, or as leaves of the entity-id index, with the empty key, when the Selection matches no index; both sides state that representation in the scope, so both hash the same items. That is the one place a digest is kept per predicate, and it is a single sum, not a tree, costing, per result change, at most a curve hash plus a subtraction and an addition. The small exchange is those two digests, 64 bytes; equal means identical ids and identical heads across the two results, so neither is fresher, and the check repeats as often as they like because nothing is rebuilt. When the digests differ, the encode of step 7, run over the two result sets, names exactly the entities that differ, at about 340 bytes each, in one round trip, and for each one the heads decide: equal, behind, ahead, or one side lacks it. One case is special and the encode surfaces it: an entity in Alice's result and not in Bob's may be one Bob never held, or one that is no longer blue on Bob's side because he holds a newer event. A head exchange tells whether Bob holds it and whose head is newer, divergent heads being settled through the event DAG as everywhere, but a head alone cannot say whether the entity is still blue: so Bob sends the newer events or state even though the entity has left his result, Alice re-evaluates it locally and drops it if it fails, and that is how a stale predicate result gets corrected without a rescan (the repair rule in the note on live queries below). What no exchange can tell is whether a third node holds something newer than both; that needs a holder that promises coverage of a branch, which is a member's claim at the branch grain (step 11), and belongs to the deferred infectious-knowledge work.

The precedent is nostr's Negentropy (NIP-77), which syncs "the events matching a filter" between a client and a relay, neither of which is complete, by comparing a fingerprint over each side's filtered set and then localizing the differences. What this design adds over it is the head, which turns "different" into "fresher or staler".

What to take from the picture. A predicate's result set can carry its own digest because it is already maintained incrementally, and a digest is enough for equality. Everything beyond equality, who is fresher and by which events, comes from the heads once the encode has named the differing entities.

Asked while reading: can Alice know she is in sync offline, from one broadcast by Bob?

The sharper form of the previous question: not coming into sync, but knowing whether one is already in sync, offline, on the basis of a one-directional transmission from Bob, without Bob having to check every query of Alice's.

Yes, with a boundary. What Bob can send once, one way, without knowing any of Alice's queries, is the node digests of his trees, which is exactly what residency claims are in step 11: scope, holder, digest, incarnation, revision, coverage, expiry, signature. For any query whose result is a disjoint union of the ranges Bob advertised, Alice answers offline by comparing her own digests for those ranges with the sum of Bob's, because the additive digest of a disjoint union is the sum. Overlapping ranges would count their shared leaves twice, and a range narrower than anything Bob advertised has no digest in the broadcast, since top-level digests cannot reveal a child's, so there the answer is "unknown". Every answer is relative to the version Bob's claims state: it says that Alice matched the digests Bob's claims state, not that either is current now, and since claims steer and never decide (step 11), an offline match is a reason to skip a session, not proof. Bob does no per-query work, his broadcast is a few kilobytes per index tree for the top levels, and any number of Alices check any number of queries against the same broadcast. Two refinements: Bob can append a fixed budget of coded symbols per prefix (the rateless stream of step 7 cut at k symbols), so that when fewer than about k/1.4 items differ, a changed entity being two of them, Alice usually decodes which ones from the broadcast alone, and otherwise the broadcast did not decode, which says nothing certain about how many differ, so she asks for more symbols or reconciles interactively (a decode tells her which items differ, not who is fresher, because the items carry head hashes; freshness waits for the heads, fetched in the session that follows); and a range in an ordered key is a handful of aligned blocks summed (the cover), so a range is answered wherever Bob's broadcast reaches those blocks. The limit: a query that is not a prefix of any index tree Bob broadcast cannot be decided from a query-agnostic summary smaller than his store, by information, since such a query can single out any entity and an exact answer for every query needs every leaf. What Alice still gets there is one-sided: equal whole-store digests mean every query is in sync, and the whole-store symbols, when the differences are few, name the differing entities, so she can say "out of sync for sure" when one is in her result and "in sync unless one of the entities I lack matches" otherwise. Certainty for arbitrary queries needs Bob evaluating them or the store itself, which is why "which indexes are hashed" is the lever: hash the keys the application's live queries are keyed by. Once Alice has detected that she lacks something, the repair is either the two-way session of step 6 or step 7, or waiting for Bob to transmit the data without being asked, which is the slower route and the natural one for a node that keeps broadcasting what it holds.

What to take from the picture. A claim is a broadcast of digests, and digests of disjoint ranges compose by addition, so one broadcast answers, for every reader, every query whose result is a disjoint union of the ranges it advertises, relative to the version it claims; elsewhere the answer is unknown. The queries it cannot answer are the ones no index tree names, which is a choice of which indexes carry trees, and the ones narrower than what Bob chose to broadcast, which is a choice of how deep to broadcast; neither is a limit of the scheme.

Asked while reading: trees on ephemeral nodes too, and one mechanism for every interest?

The proposal, 2026-10-05, in Daniel's words: keep trees on ephemeral nodes too, since "it gives us options for improving the ephemeral sync later, and simplifies the behavior by removing the differentiation. if livequeries always have indexes, then that means that we can use partial trees for sync, just with the stated prefix/range"; a partial replica should work the same way, "very similar to if the partial replica just had a durable subscription for all those records (just minus the LiveQuery driving it)", possibly the same mechanism; and "full replica just means it uses the id index with a true predicate / zero length prefix".

Yes, and it simplifies more than it costs. Trees on ephemeral nodes: an ephemeral node keeps one tree per index over whatever it holds, like everyone else, and the rule that says what can be compared is Daniel's: every node at and below a subscribed prefix is comparable, nothing above or beside it ever is. Comparable means that the two sides agree what the prefix covers, so its digests are meaningful to compare; it never means that either copy is complete. Registration makes a prefix comparable, but it does not prove that an initial fill finished or that an offline subscriber stayed complete, so each side tracks, per registered scope, whether its initial fill finished, the identity of the source it filled from and of the registration it serves, and its coverage status, which becomes complete only after a successful close following the fill (step 9) and falls back when a gap becomes known, such as a reset or a connection lost while deliveries were flowing. A result-only subscription over a residual, or a limited Selection, makes its result comparable through a tree of its own, whose root stands for the result, never the prefix beneath it. The rule holds for a durable or an ephemeral node, a full or a partial replica (a full replica's subscribed prefix is the root, so everything), and per live query, since each live query names its own prefix. Three consequences come free with it. Between two parties over the same index, the same KeySpec and key encoding, the comparison runs at and below the longer of their prefixes when one extends the other, and nowhere when neither does; with a full replica, everything at and below the other side's prefix. A range on an ordered key, or a set of values, decomposes into a handful of aligned blocks, bit prefixes of the key, as a segment tree would (the cover, in the note on it below), and the rule applies to each. And a node a side does not store still counts: with depth per branch, a side whose tree is shallower computes the digest of any prefix below its bucket from the leaves, so comparable means "can produce the digest", not "keeps a row". A state snapshot carries its head, so the leaf is computable even where the events are absent (though, as step 2 says, an equal head then certifies the snapshot's state only as far as the snapshot is trusted), and the cost is small where it matters, since a live query's result set is small and the curve hash runs fine in a browser. A live query with an index is a partial interest: when the predicate pushes down whole to an index, with no limit, its result set is "every entity under this prefix or range", the same thing a partial replica's keep rule says, so catch-up after a gap is the ordinary session rooted at that prefix, with a 64-byte check when nothing changed; a predicate that pushes down to no index gets a tree of its own over its result instead (the note on the tree spec below). And one mechanism for all four: a member's keep rule is an interest, which is any ankql Selection with a lifetime (corrected later the same morning, see the last note of this step: the Selection is the interest, and whether an existing tree serves it is a separate, implementation question). A full replica's interest is everything, served by the root of the entity-id tree; a partial replica's is a Selection it promises to cover; a durable subscription's is a Selection registered and persisted on the serving node; a live query's is a Selection registered for a session. What differs is only how long the registration lives, whether the holder promises coverage, and whether repair sends states or events. The broadcast tier sketched the night before is this mechanism with the registration broadcast instead of requested. Filed as a proposed decision in the Ankurah fwd project; the rider "no trees on ephemeral nodes" in the note under step 4 is withdrawn.

Asked while reading: does this work for live queries, and how do spilled predicates fit?

The questions, 2026-10-05: does the comparability rule clarify that this could work for live queries as well, and how would spilled predicates work?

Live queries. A live query whose predicate pushes down whole to an index, with no limit, is an interest at that prefix, so the rule applies to it directly: the client and the serving node compare at and below the prefix, and catch-up after a gap is the session rooted there. One consequence tightens the earlier note on live-query freshness: when the predicate is exactly a prefix, or a few disjoint ones, and the index files no entity twice within them, the per-result running digest is unnecessary, because the prefix node's digest already is the result's digest. That holds only because both sides state the index's key-bound leaves as the result's representation in the scope (the earlier note on live-query freshness): a reused tree's block digests and a tree of its own built with the same leaf representation are the same numbers, and a digest built from entity-id-index leaves is a different one. A tree of its own is for predicates the index cannot name, and for limits, which brings in the spill.

Spilled predicates. A spilled (residual) predicate is the part the planner cannot push into the index and evaluates on the fetched rows instead: in "genre = jazz and title contains blue", the first conjunct names the prefix and the second spills. The result is a subset of the prefix, so if the client holds only the result, its subtree at "jazz" is incomplete and the rule no longer applies there. Two honest ways out, and the design should allow both. Subscribe to the prefix: the client holds all of jazz and applies the residual as a local view; comparability at and below jazz holds, the tree does all the work, and other predicates over jazz can be evaluated offline; the cost is storage for everything under the prefix, which is fine when most of it passes the residual. Subscribe to the result: the client holds only what passes; the serving node already evaluates the residual over jazz to drive the registered query, so it feeds the result's own tree from the registered query's membership changes, and the client keeps its own; that tree's root stands for the result, equal roots meaning in sync and differing roots meaning descent, or the encode over the two result sets, and the repair below. This is the general case, the one the earlier note on live-query freshness described, now with its proper scope. The pushdown part always names the prefix; the residual's selectivity decides which of the two is cheaper, and that choice can be automatic from the ratio of the result to the prefix. Both fit the same frame: a prefix-shaped interest is a comparable subtree of an index tree, a registered predicate's result is a comparable tree of its own.

Repair of a Selection. Repair follows one rule for every Selection, whether an index tree or a tree of its own serves it. The side that holds the newer data sends updates for every entity the receiver already holds, events to a durable node and states to an ephemeral one, even when the update fails the Selection, and it sends the entities its own result holds that the receiver lacks. The receiver re-evaluates result membership locally, by its own evaluation of the Selection, so an entity that changed out of the result is dropped when its update arrives, and an entity displaced from a limited result by a better-ranked one is dropped when the better-ranked one arrives; nobody announces a removal for the other side to obey. A limited Selection's result is ordered by the Selection's own order by, with the entity id as the deterministic tie-break; only a Selection with no order by takes the serving index's order, or the entity-id order when no index serves it. So two sides holding the same entities cut the limit at the same entity, and a result that lost members is refilled from the diff, which names the entities that now rank within the limit. Fetching states and events outside the current result is allowed for this, in both directions: the serving side sends updates for what left its result, and the receiver fetches what is about to enter its own. A result digest that matches after this repair is the close of step 6 for that Selection.

Said differently: the interest mechanism in Daniel's words, and the cover

Daniel's restatement, 2026-10-05, adopted as the rule: "the specification of the digest tree must exactly match a given ankql Selection (predicate, limit, order by etc) OR prefix range if there is no spill, limit, order by that differs from the index order. the digest tree head [a better term is wanted] should specify a list of digest tree nodes, not just one. whole nodes should only be included if they precisely match the prefix range, else child nodes should be recursed. a full replica simply uses a prefix of b'' on the primary index and the digest tree, and the root node id is used. a partial replica may specify any arbitrary ankql Selection, and a tree will be created or reused as appropriate. one durable digest tree is maintained per query index, and temporary digest trees are created and freed as appropriate, given that a partial replica peer is a durable relationship, and a livequery is an ephemeral relationship (perhaps the digest tree is kept with some timeout), and when we later implement durable livequeries, that will be treated accordingly."

Four refinements, none of which change that shape. The split rule is the reuse condition, not the definition of an interest (Daniel's correction, in the last note of this step): an existing index tree serves the Selection exactly when the Selection's result has an exact, data-independent representation as a range of that index's key, which is when the predicate pushes down whole with no residual and no limit and the index files no entity twice within the range, since a Selection returns each entity once while a range digest counts every key-and-entity pair; anything else gets a tree of its own over its result. A limit always prevents reuse even when the order matches the index, because the range it cuts has a data-dependent edge that moves as entities arrive, and order by alone never prevents it, because ordering does not change the set. A term for the list: the cover. "Head" collides with the entity head, and "comparator" names the wrong thing. Superseded on 2026-10-11 by the head array (the glossary entry Cover and the step 13 row): the opener states the span it compares as node heads of its own tree with bounds where the span cuts a node, and a count becomes a stated bound. The decomposition below is kept as the earlier design. The cover of an interest was a canonical logical decomposition of its key range into aligned binary blocks, the dyadic intervals over the key's bits: a block is every key that starts with a given bit string, included whole when it fits inside the range and split in two where it straddles an edge, down to the bits of the bounds. That is the list of nodes in Daniel's restatement made independent of any store: the blocks depend on the range bounds alone, never on a store's depth, fan-out or stored nodes, so two stores whose trees differ still derive the same blocks. For a prefix the cover is one block, for the full replica it is the root, for a range it is a handful, at most two per bit of the bounds. The side that opens a session exchanges the index identity, the index's KeySpec and the canonical key encoding, and the range bounds explicitly, rather than trusting both planners to choose the same index and both stores to tile alike; each side then answers the digest of every block from its own rows or leaves, adding the stored nodes that lie inside the block and scanning leaves where it keeps none. A session asks for the digests of all the blocks in one message and descends below any that differ. Trees created or reused: a Selection that reduces to a prefix range on an existing index reuses that index's durable tree; on an index that does not exist yet, the index and its tree are created together; a Selection that does not reduce gets a tree of its own over its result, built from the result at registration and maintained afterwards from the registered query's membership changes, with the result as the reactor maintains it, limit included, as its leaf set. A tree of its own is the same tree as any other (the project lead's ruling of 2026-10-08: one fingerprint for every case): the same leaf hashing, the same fold, the same cover; it stays a root-only tree while small, since buckets split only past their target; and it stores its leaves as key-bound leaves of the index the interest names in its scope, each entity's canonical key computed from its state under that KeySpec, or as entity-id-index leaves, with the empty key, when the Selection matches no index, so a reused tree's block digests and a tree of its own built with the same representation are the same numbers. What differs between trees is only where each lives and for how long, and both follow its owner, with no flag: a tree owned by an index lives in the engine as long as the index; a tree owned by a live query lives in memory as long as the subscription, fed by the reactor after each commit, and is rebuilt from the result when the subscription starts; a tree owned by a partial replica peer or a durable subscription lives in the engine as long as that relationship, recorded in the registry of durable trees (the appendix), so it survives a restart. The threshold above which a memory-resident tree spills to the engine is open (ruling pending).

The tree spec. What defines a tree is its tree spec, ruled on 2026-10-08 and settled on 2026-10-11: the key spec that addresses its leaves, over explicit property ids, and the whole membership predicate, and nothing else. The two fields divide into ordering and admission. The key spec is the ordering: it fixes every leaf's address and so the prefixes the nodes are named by. It carries no directions and no nulls placement: every part is encoded ascending, with nulls at one fixed end, so every order over the same parts names one tree, and the index on (a, b) in any directions, ORDER BY a ASC, b DESC and its reverse all read the one tree keyed (a, b). Which way a use walks each part, and where nulls fall for it, is the use's order, a key spec with directions that the use keeps beside its scope. A digest is a sum, so the order changes no digest; it decides only which leaves fall under a limit's cut, and that cut is a few spans of the one tree: the tiers before the cut whole, then within the cut's tier the leaves past the cut value in the walk's direction, then within the cut's own value the entity ids up to it. The scan reverses per tier, forward over a's tiers and backward inside each for b DESC. The predicate is the admission: the Selection's whole predicate, resolved, with any membership term the core supplies; none for an index tree, which files every entity under its key when all key parts are present. There is no component in it, by the project lead's word: an index is over entity data, keyed by property ids that may be shared among models. There is no limit in it either, and no range: what a use compares is its scope, which the use keeps in the core beside the tree it uses, never the spec or the registry: the span of the tree it reads, stated as an array of node heads, the order it walks them in, and the count it takes under a limit (the note on the cover below). The key spec of a Selection's tree is derived from the Selection alone, by one rule every member applies and never from the indexes a member happens to have, or two members would hold two trees for one Selection: the parts the predicate pins by equality, then the ORDER BY parts when the Selection has a limit, or the single range part when it has none; a limit-less ORDER BY never enters a spec, since it changes no membership, so the tree has no order-by spill of its own. When the predicate is nothing but those equalities and that range, the tree is the index tree and the Selection is a scope over it, the many-to-one direction; otherwise a tree of its own with the same key spec and the whole predicate serves every Selection that shares those two, whatever its range, direction or limit. Whether a tree is an index tree is a fact about its owner, recorded in the registry, not a property of the spec. The spec, with the canonical key encoding's version, is the identity two members compare before reusing a tree; it replaces the earlier "hashed index", which named the wrong thing twice over: nothing hashes the index, and a tree is not only over an index. Whichever serves a Selection, its repair follows the rule in the note on live queries above: updates reach every entity the receiver holds even when they fail the Selection, the receiver re-evaluates locally, and a limited result, ordered by the Selection's own order by with the entity id breaking ties, or by the serving index's order only when the Selection has none, is refilled from the diff.

Corrected: an interest is any Selection; the only question is reuse

Daniel, 2026-10-05: "I don't see the point of making an Interest any less specific than any arbitrary ankql::Selection. why 'when the Selection pushes down whole'? either way an interest is a Selection isn't it. The only question is when can I use just a sub portion of an already existent digest tree vs rebuilding a whole new one."

Agreed, and it is a cleaner cut. An interest is any ankql Selection, nothing less: predicate, order by, limit, registered for some lifetime, a session for a live query, persisted for a partial replica peer or a durable subscription, the whole store for a full replica. What the earlier notes called the split rule is not part of the definition; it is the reuse condition. Reuse an existing tree when the Selection's result is a range of that index's key, which holds when the predicate pushes down whole to the index with no residual and no limit and the index files no entity twice within the range: then the digests of the cover's blocks, answered from that tree's rows and leaves, sum to the Selection's digest and nothing is built. Order by alone never prevents reuse, because ordering does not change the set; a limit does, because the edge it cuts moves with the data, which corrects the "order by that differs from the index order" clause of the restatement above. Otherwise build a tree of its own over the result: built once at registration from the result, maintained afterwards by the registered Selection's membership changes, root-only while it is small, and freed when the registration ends plus its timeout. So the only question is the one named, and its answer is: reuse requires an exact, data-independent representation of the result as an index range, with no residual and no limit. A union of single-leaf ranges can describe any finite result, spilled and limited ones included, so only a range fixed by the Selection and the index, never by the data, qualifies.

What a member keeps and compares: any ankql Selection (predicate, order by, limit) with a lifetime. A full replica’s interest is everything; a partial replica’s, a durable subscription’s and a live query’s are Selections with different lifetimes. An existing index tree serves an interest, through a scope over it, exactly when the Selection’s predicate is nothing but equalities and a range on that index’s key, a limit served as a count when the key continues with the Selection’s ORDER BY, and no entity filed twice within the range; otherwise a tree of its own with the derived key spec and the whole predicate serves it, shared with every Selection that has the same two.
Cover
The span of a tree a comparison covers, stated by the opener as an array of node heads: node prefixes of its own tree, with a key bound where the span cuts a node, [root] for a whole tree. A count under a limit becomes the bound the opener states from its stored counts. A session exchanges the tree identity and the head array; each side answers every head’s digest from its own rows or leaves, whatever its own layout, and descends below any that differ. Supersedes the aligned-block decomposition of 2026-10-05.
Member
A durable node admitted to hold a system, a pinned part of it, or a share of it. Ephemeral nodes are not members, but they keep trees over whatever they hold and compare at and below their subscribed prefixes like any member (the note on ephemeral nodes under step 12).
Group
The set of entities one share grants: everything filed under one root entity, such as a project. Its id, the group id, is that root entity’s id; it is what the index keyed by group then entity carries in front of each entity’s own id, authored on the entity under entity tagging or derived by the serving node on the Zanzibar path.
Head
The set of an entity's newest events, the ones no later event names as a parent.
Leaf
The canonical key under which an index files an entity, the entity id, and the hash of the entity’s sorted head: the unit a session compares. An index holds one leaf per distinct key-and-entity pair, enforced by the store, so an entity under two keys is two leaves; on the entity-id index the key is empty and the entity id alone orders the leaves.
Node
A group of leaves that share a key prefix, carrying a digest and a leaf count, which add and subtract as leaves change: a cached sum over the key range the prefix names, kept as a base row and the delta rows appended since its last compaction. Nodes are derived state with derived names, never entities or events.
Bucket
The leaves under the deepest prefix a store keeps a node row for, a few dozen to a few hundred leaves.
Digest
The sum, on an elliptic curve, of the points of every leaf beneath a node. Equal digests with equal counts mean equal leaves, under the assumptions of step 4 and with every leaf unique.
The commit’s fold
Core’s update of the trees inside every commit that wrote states, on the same engine transaction as the entity rows and before COMMIT: it derives the leaves, reads the old folded rows, subtracts the old points and adds the new ones along their paths, writes the folded rows conditionally on what it read and appends the node deltas, so entity rows and tree rows are visible together or not at all. Not a hook: the engine calls into core only where it creates or drops an index.
Index hooks
The engine’s calls into core where it creates an index and where it drops one; core registers the index’s tree and builds it, or removes it.
Tree spec
What defines a tree: the key spec that addresses its leaves, over explicit property ids and without directions, every part encoded ascending, and the whole membership predicate; no component, no limit, no range. An index tree’s spec has no predicate; a tree of its own carries its Selection’s whole predicate under the key spec derived from the Selection by one rule on every member. With the key encoding’s version, it is the identity two members compare before reusing a tree. What a use compares is its scope, kept by the use: a head array, the order it walks, and a count.
Tree of its own
The tree built over a Selection’s result when no index tree serves it: the same tree as any other, root-only while small, owned by the live query, partial replica peer or durable subscription that registered it, living in memory or in the engine and for as long as its owner does.
Registry
The system model whose entities record each durable tree a node keeps: its spec, its status, its engine index identity and the node that holds it. The tree service follows it through a live query and combines it with the ephemeral trees in memory.
Session
One reconciliation between two members, owned by the side that opened it: the scope, then roots, then children of differing nodes, then buckets, then events, then the refold and the closing check of every root of the scope at full width.
Descent
Opening differing nodes one level at a time until a bucket is reached.
Rateless encode
A stream of coded symbols, each combining a pseudo-random subset of the sender’s fixed-size leaf items (key hash, entity id, head hash) with a signed count and a keyed checksum, produced until the receiver has decoded every difference or reached its cap; no size is fixed in advance, and a stream that stops at the cap did not decode.
Claim
A statement that a holder holds a named scope at a digest, with its incarnation and a revision number that orders its own claims for that scope, the coverage it asserts and an expiry, signed once the attested design binds keys to holders. Claims steer; digest exchanges decide.
Share
A grant from one durable node to a peer of a named set of entities: the peer, the rights, a root and the definition by which membership is decided.
Serving node
The one durable node on the granting side that serves a share and maintains the derived rows that put each entity under its group.

Appendix: what the storage engine contract gains

A sketch against the contract at the current main (core/src/storage.rs: a transaction with add_events, set_state and commit; an engine with get_state, fetch_states, get_events and the catalog hooks), the planner's exact-or-prefix match of a Selection against a KeySpec, the engine-independent, order-preserving index key encoding in core/src/indexing/encoding.rs, and the engines' auto-creation of indexes from a query's plan (sled's and IndexedDB's assure-index-exists, with their backfill scans). The split that keeps the cryptography in one reviewable place: core owns the digest mathematics and the fold, the engines own ordered storage and the transaction. Rewritten on 2026-10-08 to the project lead's rulings of that morning.

  • The core adds a crate below it, ankurah-digest-tree, generic over the key spec and the predicate: the tree spec, the prefix addressing, the folded and node rows, an ordered byte map trait with an in-memory implementation, the fold over leaf changes, the build from a set of leaves, the audit, the head array over byte bounds that a scope states, and the tree service that holds trees by spec, memory-resident first, with the registration source of durable trees injected. Nothing in the crate reads entity state, evaluates a predicate, encodes a key or opens a transaction. Core itself keeps what needs an entity's state: deriving keys and evaluating predicates for the fold, the canonical key encoding, the head hash from the clock, the registry system model and its live query, the hooks, and the binding to the engine's transaction; the reuse decision, Selection to tree spec and scope, stays with the planner. No engine sees a curve.
  • The commit gains tree writes on its own transaction, and no callback. Core's commit path already holds the engine's transaction for the entity rows, events, memberships and materializations; before COMMIT it issues the tree writes on that transaction, conditional row writes and delta appends in the vocabulary of the ordered byte map below, which the engine buffers with the commit's own writes and applies at COMMIT. A conflict on an expected head returns before any tree write; a failed condition on a tree row fails the commit as a conflict, retried from fresh reads; an error rolls the commit back. A commit that sets a state without storing an event folds too, and so does every change that moves a leaf without a commit on the entity, a derived group membership change on the Zanzibar path or a catalog or relation change, in the same transaction as the change. The commit log rows, their read path, the stable position, the refresher, the per-store commit position and the counter row that would have minted it are withdrawn; a store's incarnation, minted at creation and at reset, stays, for first contact and claims.
  • Each engine gains tree storage: per registered tree, four ordered maps keyed by bytes (folded rows by canonical key then entity id, one per key-and-entity pair; the lookup from entity id to its keys, so the fold finds the old leaf it must subtract or move; node rows by prefix, a base row and the delta rows appended beneath it; and the tree's build state), written only inside a commit's transaction by core's fold, by a build, or by compaction. Reads: a row by key and a bounded range scan, which give an entity's keys, the children of a prefix, a node's base and deltas, the leaves of a bucket or a key range, and a forward scan of the folded rows for the encode. Writes: insert-if-absent, put-if-current-equals and delete-if-current-equals, each failing as a conflict when its condition does not hold, and an append under a key prefix with a suffix the engine makes unique to that append. Points pass through the core's storage codec, validated when read and versioned, so a change of encoding rebuilds or migrates the rows. In IndexedDB the maps are object stores created once and keyed by tree id, since a store per tree would mean a version change per index.
  • Index creation and deletion fire hooks. Where an engine creates an index it calls core with the index in core's terms, the key spec over property ids, and its own index identity; core registers one tree per key spec and builds it from entity state, from which the keys derive, with each entity's head (how membership enters that key, as sled's leading materialization column does, is the discussion task filed on 2026-10-08, since every engine materializes members only), and the build runs under the same lock the engine's index build takes, sled's mutation lock, the lock a SQL engine's index creation holds against writers, IndexedDB's version change, as the same scan where the index is auto-created, so commits wait for it and no leaf is added twice or added back; the tree is published as ready when the build ends. Where an engine drops an index it fires the other hook, and core removes the tree and its rows. Batched builds with per-batch entity locks are a later optimization (ruled 2026-10-11, filed as a task). One tree per index, the entity-id index without exception, opt-out per index as ruled on 2026-10-05. The declared-key-spec alternative, trees registered from the schema independently of the engines' indexes, was proposed by the lead and withdrawn the same day.
  • Durable trees have a registry: a system model, like the catalog's models and properties, whose entities record each durable tree a node keeps, its spec, its status, its engine index identity and the node that holds it, written by core when an index hook fires or a durable interest registers. The tree service holds a live query over that model, injected so the service builds and tests without it, which yields the initial set at startup and the registrations another writer makes on a shared store; it combines them with the ephemeral trees in memory. Whether registry entities are node-local or replicated carrying the node's identity is a ruling pending; the lead leans to replicated, so a session learns a partner's trees without asking.
  • Trees of their own. A Selection no index tree serves gets a tree over its result, owned by the live query, partial replica peer or durable subscription that registered it. A live query's tree is memory-resident, fed by the reactor after each commit from the result it already maintains, limit included, rebuilt from the result when the subscription starts and lost with the process; a partial replica's or a durable subscription's lives in the engine like an index tree, recorded in the registry, so it survives a restart. The threshold above which a memory-resident tree spills to the engine is open.

Order of work (the Digest trees scope in fwd, filed on 2026-10-08): the crate, memory-resident, in six steps: the skeleton with the tree spec, the prefixes, the rows and the in-memory store; the fold; the build and the audit; the cover; the service; the rewire of the reuse decision and the removal of the replaced core code. Then durable trees: the storage contract with the ordered byte map, its conditional writes and the append; the index hooks; the registry and the injected live query; SQLite; sled; PostgreSQL with its conflict rate under concurrent commits measured; IndexedDB. The replication chain follows on those: the session between two durable nodes over a cover, the encode and first contact, live queries, partial replicas, claims. Each step is reviewed by a second model and then line by line by the project lead before it merges.

Tests. The deterministic two-store simulation is acceptance work still to build, and it checks each store against an independent oracle, a direct model of events and Selections, never against the stores' own folded rows. It exercises different arrival orders, duplicate messages, partitions, divergent heads, different tree depths and fan-outs, mixed encode and descent, key moves, limits, residuals, interrupted sessions, and a third member's commits for live delivery. Crash injection surrounds every step: the commit with its fold, so that after a restart the state and the tree rows are found together or not at all; splits, compaction and index builds under concurrent commits, with index builds also cancelled, removed and re-created; and remote acknowledgments; after each restart, heads, folded keys and points, and node digests and counts are compared with independently recovered state, since an audit that reads only the folded rows cannot see a folded table that is consistently stale. Cross-engine vectors cover composite keys, missing and null values, numeric boundaries, strings, head permutations, entities under several keys and index moves, and compare canonical leaf bytes, compressed points, digests of arbitrary prefixes and ranges, and Selection results across all four engines, with different local tree shapes and codec versions, since matching roots alone can miss a key-binding mistake. Decode measurements state their stopping budget, record symbol-budget and work-budget exhaustion apart from wrong decodes, and cover tiny differences, two items per changed entity, asymmetric sets, adversarial checksums and mappings, and forced short-fingerprint collisions, reporting tail symbol counts, memory, fallback rate and the end-to-end full-width check. The size rule's thresholds are recomputed once that final mechanism runs.
